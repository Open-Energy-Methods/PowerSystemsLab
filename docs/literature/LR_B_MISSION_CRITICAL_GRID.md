# LR-B — Critical infrastructure and MissionCriticalGrid

## 1. Scope and application
The first application is a synthetic 24 MW IT campus, 16 blocks, double 110 kV connection and sectionalized 20 kV network. It is not a real facility or consultancy design. ENTSO-E's 2026 report [R20, pp.6,9] distinguishes colocation, hyperscale and enterprise sites and describes hyperscale sites as frequently above 50 MW IT. Our case is best called a synthetic data-centre campus or a hypothetical stage of a larger campus, not evidence of a typical hyperscale size. No expansion is necessary for the learning goal.

ENTSO-E's architecture illustration separates mechanical power and IT UPS paths, and treats IT workload, converter interfaces and cooling as materially different grid-facing loads. Current equal A/B split is an optimistic capacity allocation; it is not a service-level topology. Expand design basis with independent utility routes (or explicit common dependencies), transformer and MV section isolation, generators and fuel, UPS rectifier/inverter/bypass and batteries, cooling feeders, auxiliary/control power and transfer dependencies. Separate electrical availability from service continuity and redundancy from maintainability. The Uptime overview [R23] establishes certification as a separate process; no Tier designation follows from 41 synthetic outage cases.

## 2. Demand, losses and PUE
The ISO catalogue confirms a new 2026 edition of the PUE metric [R22]. It is an energy KPI, not an instantaneous load-model prescription. At a specified design point, calculate input IT conversion loss from efficiency, cooling and auxiliaries from explicit component assumptions, separate real and reactive demand, and add solved electrical losses once. PUE requires energy integration and defined measurement boundaries, not a nominal 1.3 multiplication followed by additional loss accounting.

The current 24 MW IT model has assumed 97% UPS efficiency, 0.34 MW cooling and 0.05 MW auxiliary demand per block, yielding approximately 30.982 MW before network losses. These are case inputs, not literature-derived values. Obtain a representative efficiency-versus-load curve and PF, recharge strategy, bypass state, motor/VFD load composition and ambient-dependent cooling demand. Use separate P/Q models for each component. Sensitivity cases must reflect uncertainty rather than present assumed inputs as measured values. The detailed block has two LV transformers while 15 other blocks omit their downstream losses: current reported losses are only the represented network's losses.

## 3. Restoration and contingency interpretation
The existing workflow reports 124 converged states across 41 single-component outages plus baseline. In the optimistic restoration model all 24 MW IT are assignable. The limiting MV-section loss result is 0.95153 pu (only 0.00153 pu above the assumed 0.95 screen). This is narrow model-dependent margin, not an operational limit verified against grid rules. Prioritize sensitivity to source voltage, transformer tap/impedance, load composition and feeder impedance. Compute service limits with explicit transfer capability per load component. Add unavailable breaker/UPS/generator/ATS, maintenance and common-mode events; track critical load shed and restoration times separately. Treat dead-section isolation and tie closure as abstract states, not an approved operating sequence.

The ASHRAE handbook [R21] notes that duplicated UPS power does not necessarily imply duplicated cooling. A thermal model should therefore account for IT heat, active cooling, effective thermal masses, coolant and pump availability, ambient conditions, allowable equipment temperatures, and restart sequencing. A simple lumped energy balance is a sensitivity model unless thermal data support it. Do not transfer a published typical backup duration to this facility. UPS energy balance must include efficiency, available capacity/temperature and aging; generator step-load and cooling restart require dynamics, not just a steady-state MW margin.

## 4. Standby generation
The previously reviewed STAMFORD alternator rev E has a newer public rev F (9 September 2025) [R12]. Printed pp.2,3,6 retain the values relevant to our 400 V/50 Hz, 2500 kVA reactance base. Saturated Xd=2.36, X'd=.16, X''d=.12 pu; transient/subtransient time constants .164/.008 s; separately excited sustained 3ph current 11.142 kA. The curve is specified under no-load excitation at rated speed and star connection; voltage correction applies to the earlier segment, not the sustained current. It shows decay followed by recovery. The simple fixed-field model used in the project has a lower long-time asymptote, so it cannot establish generator-mode protection sensitivity by itself. Manufacturer curve and excitation assumptions matter.

The representative machine is not the proposed 3.125 MVA genset. A matched 50 Hz 400 V alternator/engine package and rating category, prime mover inertia, governor, AVR, limits, ambient derating and load-step curves must be obtained. Compare generator kW and kVA, not merely one rating. Alternator-only inertia can support a teaching case but not full shaft inertia. Do not combine data from voltage/frequency columns or assume a curve for one alternator applies to another.

## 5. Equipment, short circuit and protection
See LR-A and LR-C. [R08] provides an independent sequence-network case and illustrates separate fault-current quantities. [R05] and [R07] reveal the edition distinction. For utility-fed study collect maximum/minimum source strength and R/X, actual cable temperature and positive/zero sequence data, transformer vector groups, zero-sequence impedances, grounding path and configuration. Consider motor and converter fault contribution by operating mode, with explicit exclusions. Use native outputs only within version-supported scope. Current transformer-only stiff-source estimate is a nominal illustration, not a design duty.

The Schneider coordination guide [R11] has candidate families, but exact rated voltage, frame/trip unit, long/short/instantaneous characteristics, tolerances, breaker time and tested combinations have not been extracted. W5 should first map protection zones (HV/MV transformer differential and backup, MV phase/earth overcurrent, LV main ACB and feeder MCCB, generator protection, UPS bypass), then grade a representative path with device currents and inrush/withstand constraints. Selectivity in high-current current-limiting regions requires tested vendor combinations. No claim of equipment coordination, arc-flash safety or code compliance until applicable data/method are qualified.

## 6. Grid-facing behaviour
ENTSO-E [R20] identifies distinct IT and cooling dynamics and the role of UPS and power-electronic cooling interfaces. NERC's large-load review [R24] highlights observability, ride-through, stability and data needs. NERC is North American, not a binding German standard. Treat the campus as a large load with a separate point-of-connection model: workload ramps, voltage/frequency thresholds, disconnection and recovery, power factor/reactive controls, and converter-limited response. Static demand is appropriate only for the defined load-flow screen. RMS/EMT models and measured or representative behaviour must be validated before grid-impact conclusions. R25 is an additional follow-up lead, not fully reviewed.

## 7. Design gap priorities
P0: standard edition and scope; source and sequence model; CT158 provenance; representative actual equipment curves; generator model boundary. P1: component load registry, voltage-margin sensitivity, all-block downstream losses, switching/transfer hardware, UPS and cooling continuity. P2: dynamic grid-facing load, harmonics, energy optimization, probabilistic/common-mode reliability, broader grid connection. See decision register for closure criteria. No publication of commercial outputs without permissions. This initial review is not a complete data-centre engineering survey; independent empirical/field cases and detailed vendor studies must be added.
